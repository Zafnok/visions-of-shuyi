## At a glance

"Gil Parrow, Champion of Six Fairs." He won the bare-knuckle ring at six
country fairs in a row before the war, and introduces himself with the
whole title every time. The war closed the fairs, and since then he has
fought in inn yards for bets. On the Millhaven road he has bet his last
purse that he can beat a whole Hound patrol, because a Hound kicked over
a pie-seller's cart in front of him and his code forbids walking past
that.

He lives by a code: numbered rules about fighting, eating, debts and
manners, absurd in detail and kept to the letter. He invents new ones
when he needs them and insists they are old. By his code, a beaten man
serves the one who beat him until he has won a fair rematch. The lead
beats him. So he follows the lead, and waits, very patiently, for the
right day to ask.

## Want, need, flaw, secret

- **Want:** his rematch with the lead, on a fitting day, before a crowd.
  Under that, a seventh fair, won honestly.
- **Need:** to see that the code is a penance, not an honor, and that
  keeping faith with people matters more than keeping rules.
- **Flaw:** rigid pride. He walks into a fight he can't win because rule
  four says so, and he is vain about the title.
- **Secret:** he should have been Champion of Seven. At the seventh fair,
  in Cairnford, the summer before the war, he took silver to lose, and
  lost. His young cousin, who held his coat at every fight, had bet a
  year's wages on him. The boy enlisted that autumn to pay the debt and
  died in the war's first year. Every rule in the code is a promise to
  him. Nobody alive knows.
- **Pressure:** he has no money, no fair and no ring, and a code that
  forbids him almost every way of getting any.

## Arc

- **Start:** a comic, courtly giant with a rule for everything, who
  follows the lead because a rule says to.
- **End of Act 1:** he stays because he wants to. He says so, and it
  costs him a rule.
- **Acts 2–3:** he tells somebody about Cairnford (a support or side
  quest). He gets his rematch, or decides he doesn't want it (the act
  tickets decide which).

## Voice card

Written by 0743 (the `story-writing` skill, *Voices*). The sample lines
are illustrations of a situation, never lines for a script.

- **Age and experience:** thirty-four; a carter's son, fifteen years in
  the ring, a fairground man. He talks like the man who stood in the
  middle of the crowd and announced himself: courtly, formal, slow,
  completely serious. He knows fighting, fairs, crowds, inns, pies,
  wagers and how to bow; he reads slowly.
- **Temperature:** warm and solemn.
- **Openness:** says what he means formally and at length, except the
  one thing, which he doesn't say at all.
- **When scared:** quieter and more polite; holds very still; says a rule
  under his breath.
- **Care signature:** who is standing behind whom. He puts his body in
  front of people, sits on the windward side, takes the outside of the
  road, without remarking on it.
- **Fault the others put up with:** eats enormously; bows to everyone,
  including horses; challenges strangers to friendly bouts; announces
  himself; quotes the code.
- **Line length and habit:** long, formal sentences in downtime, with his
  title in them; the code by number ("Rule nine."). In danger, short and
  plain, and no rules at all. Calls the lead "Champion" (gender-neutral,
  and meant).
- **Words from his life:** the ring, the bell, the crowd, the purse, the
  fairs by town name, his ma's pies, the code. His cousin, never.
- **Won't say:** a bad word about anyone he has fought, or "I yield".
  Beaten, he says "Well struck."
- **Punctuation:** formal periods; a `!` in his announcing voice (once a
  scene in downtime, never in a fight); a rare `...` when the code runs
  out.

Downtime is where he is funny (`setting-and-tone.md`, *Where the jokes
go*): the joke is how seriously he takes it. In a serious battle he is
plain and brave, and the rules stay in his pocket.

**Sample lines** (one per situation; never copied into a script)

- *Under fire:* "Champion. Behind me, close. Closer. When I go down on
  one knee, you go over my back."
- *At camp:* "Rule nine: the one who cooks eats first. ...It's an old
  rule. From the fairs. It's very old."
- *Lying:* "The seventh fair? Rained off. The whole fair, the whole week.
  Terrible rain, that year, in-- somewhere. I forget where."
- *Grieving:* "He used to hold my coat. Every fight, the whole fight, and
  fold it after like church clothes. ...Somebody ought to fold his."

## Planted for after

- **Plan for after:** to hold a fair of his own. A ring, a pie stall,
  a greased pole, and one rule over the gate: no betting.
- **In his hands:** six faded fair ribbons stitched along his belt, and a
  bare place for a seventh. He touches the bare place.
